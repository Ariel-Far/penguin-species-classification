Beating the Ceiling: When Linear Models Stop Improving

Three standard classifiers plateaued at 97% on a deliberately starved feature set. A neural network written from scratch in NumPy found the 3% they couldn't.

i.The Problem
Three species, and only three features to tell them apart

The Palmer Penguins dataset records 344 birds across three species and seventeen columns. The assignment imposed a hard constraint: classify species using at most two numeric features and one categorical feature. Most of the data had to be thrown away. The real question wasn't whether classification was possible — it was how much signal survives when you are forced to choose almost nothing, and what kind of model extracts the last of it.

ii.The Method
Choose the features on evidence, then let the models argue

Feature selection came first and was decided by correlation ranking and visual separability — not by guessing. Only then did model comparison begin.

iii.The Finding That Shaped Everything
Why geography outperformed biology

Island correlated with species at 0.64 — higher than most physical measurements. That looked strange until the grouped counts explained it: Gentoo appear only on Biscoe, and Chinstrap only on Dream. Island isn't describing the bird; it is encoding where the species live, which makes it nearly a free label for two of three classes.

This is the kind of feature that flatters a model on a clean academic dataset and betrays it in the field — it would generalize to no other archipelago. Keeping it was correct under the assignment's constraint, but the reason it works is a fact about sampling, not about penguins. Naming that distinction mattered more than the accuracy score.

Where the linear models broke

Logistic regression, SVM, and LDA all cut the feature space with straight boundaries, and all three stalled around 97%. The confusion matrices showed the same failure every time: a small cluster of Adelie and Chinstrap birds overlapping in culmen space. No straight line separates them. The remaining error wasn't noise — it was the shape of the boundary.

My Contribution
I built the fourth model: a multi-layer perceptron implemented from scratch in NumPy — no scikit-learn, no PyTorch. Min–max scaling, a nine-neuron ReLU hidden layer, softmax output over three classes, cross-entropy loss, and manually derived backpropagation with gradient descent. Because its decision boundaries bend rather than cut, it resolved the overlapping Adelie–Chinstrap cases that every linear model missed, reaching ~99% test accuracy with a clean confusion matrix.

The perceptron's hyperparameters — learning rate and epoch count — were selected by inspecting training-loss curves for convergence and diminishing returns, not by grid search.

Why this is the part that matters

Reaching 99% was not the interesting outcome — knowing why 97% was the ceiling was. Three independent algorithms failing at the same boundary, on the same handful of birds, is a diagnosis rather than a disappointment: it says the remaining error is geometric, and that a curved boundary should recover it. Writing the network by hand instead of importing one meant the fix followed from the diagnosis rather than from swapping models until a number improved.

Methodology, in brief. pandas cleaning with leakage-aware train/test splitting; feature selection by correlation ranking and decision-region inspection; scikit-learn logistic regression, SVM, and LDA evaluated under k-fold cross-validation with confusion matrices; multi-layer perceptron implemented from scratch in NumPy with manual backpropagation. Group project of three — cleaning, statistics, feature selection, and the evaluation function were shared; the perceptron model and scatter-plot analysis were mine.
